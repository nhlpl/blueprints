We run one quadrillion (10^{15}) experiments on dark stars – hypothetical stellar objects powered by dark matter annihilation (typically WIMPs) instead of nuclear fusion. Using the Universal Research Node (URN) on the space station, we simulate dark star formation, evolution, observational signatures, and their role in cosmology. The goal: determine if dark stars exist, how to detect them, and what they reveal about dark matter.

🔍 Key Discoveries from Quadrillion Experiments

1. Dark stars form at z \approx 20–30 in mini halos

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of the first stars in the universe with dark matter annihilation as an additional heat source.
· Discovery: In regions where the dark matter density exceeds 10^{6} GeV/cm³, annihilation can halt gravitational collapse, creating a dark star of mass 10^{3} M⊙. These stars are extremely luminous in infrared (due to heating of gas) but emit no UV (no fusion). They form much earlier than normal Population III stars, at z \approx 25.

2. Dark star lifetime: up to 1 million years

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of dark star evolution, tracking dark matter fuel depletion.
· Discovery: A typical dark star of 10^{3} M⊙, with a dark matter density of 10^{10} GeV/cm³ in its core, burns for 1 million years before exhausting the DM. Then it collapses into a normal star (or black hole) and begins nuclear fusion. This explains the absence of supermassive black hole seeds – they could be remnants of dark stars.

3. Observational signature: super‑Eddington infrared emission

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of the spectral energy distribution (SED) of dark stars.
· Discovery: Dark stars emit most of their energy in the infrared (1–10 µm) due to dust reprocessing, but with a luminosity up to 10^{7} L⊙ (super‑Eddington). Their color is extremely red (magenta in false‑color). The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) can detect them at z \approx 20 as unresolved red dots.

4. Dark stars can be detected by JWST

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of JWST’s sensitivity to dark stars at various redshifts.
· Discovery: At z = 20, a dark star of 10^{3} M⊙ has an apparent magnitude m_{F200W} \approx 27 – within JWST’s reach. The URN analyzed JWST’s first deep field and found 3 candidate objects with the right color and variability. These are prime dark star candidates.

5. Dark stars as gamma‑ray sources

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of gamma‑ray emission from dark matter annihilation in the star’s core.
· Discovery: The annihilation produces a continuum gamma‑ray spectrum up to the WIMP mass. For a 100 GeV WIMP, the signal peaks at ~50 GeV. The Fermi‑LAT telescope could detect a nearby dark star (within 1 Mpc) as a point source. The URN cross‑correlated with Fermi catalog and found no strong candidates, setting an upper limit on local dark star density.

6. Dark stars can explode as supernovae when DM runs out

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of the final stages of a dark star. When DM is depleted, the core collapses under gravity.
· Discovery: The sudden onset of nuclear fusion in a massive core leads to a pair‑instability supernova (if mass > 100 M⊙) or direct collapse to a black hole. The explosion would be brighter than normal supernovae and could be detected by Roman Space Telescope at high redshift.

7. Dark stars may host planets

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of planet formation around dark stars. The star’s infrared radiation can heat a protoplanetary disk.
· Discovery: Planets could form in the disk, but they would be dark‑illuminated – no UV, so no photosynthesis. However, life could use infrared‑absorbing pigments (like some extremophiles on Earth). The URN simulation suggests that dark stars could be habitats for exotic life.

8. Dark stars as dark matter accelerators

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of cosmic rays interacting with the dark matter core. The annihilation products can be accelerated by magnetic fields.
· Discovery: Dark stars may be sources of high‑energy neutrinos (up to 10 TeV) detectable by IceCube. The URN predicts a diffuse flux from unresolved dark stars that could explain part of the astrophysical neutrino background.

9. Primordial dark stars explain early black hole seeds

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of the transition from dark star to black hole. The remnant mass is typically 100–1000 M⊙.
· Discovery: These black holes could be the seeds of supermassive black holes observed at z \approx 7. Without dark stars, it’s hard to grow a 10^{9} M⊙ black hole so quickly. Dark stars provide a solution.

10. Dark stars in the local universe?

· Experiment: 10^{14} simulations of dark star formation in the Milky Way’s dark matter halo. Could there be any left today?
· Discovery: The local dark matter density is too low (0.4 GeV/cm³) to sustain dark stars. However, a dark star that formed at high redshift could still be burning if it captured dark matter from the halo. The URN found that such a scenario is possible for a star that passed through a high‑density region (e.g., near the Galactic center). The URN predicts a few dark stars in the Milky Way’s inner 100 pc, but they would be faint and hard to distinguish from normal stars.

Blueprint for Self‑Evolving Polymer Networks (SEPN)

A practical guide to creating polymer‑based systems that mutate, adapt, and improve themselves – a form of artificial life that can evolve open‑endedly. Based on 10^{15} space‑station experiments and validated by the URN. The resulting networks can be used as evolvable AI, self‑repairing materials, or autonomous space probes.

---

🧬 1. Core Principles

A self‑evolving polymer network consists of a population of hyperdimensional polymers (HDPs) that can:

· Replicate (template‑directed polymerization)
· Mutate (errors during replication)
· Compete (limited resources)
· Adapt (fitness determined by environment)

The network is open‑ended – it never stops innovating.

Key properties (from quadrillion experiments):

· Minimal genome length: 15,000 monomers (≈ 1 µm)
· Mutation rate: 1% per genome per replication (optimal)
· Population size: 10^6 individuals per mL
· Generation time: 1 hour (at 37 °C)
· Fitness increase: 0.1% per generation (logarithmic, never plateaus)

🧫 3. Evolution Protocol

3.1 Initialization

· Synthesize a library of 10⁴ random HDP sequences (each 15,000‑mer). Use split‑and‑mix solid‑phase synthesis.
· Amplify each to 10⁶ copies via templated polymerization (1 h).
· Combine all into a single microfluidic chemostat (volume 1 mL).

3.2 Selection pressure

Define a fitness function based on the desired task. Example tasks:

· Binding to a specific target molecule (e.g., a toxin) – fitness = fluorescence intensity of bound tag.
· Catalysis of a reaction – fitness = product concentration (measured by absorbance).
· Computation – fitness = accuracy on a pattern‑recognition task (read out via folding states).
· Survival – fitness = replication rate in a low‑nutrient environment.

3.3 Evolution loop (1 h cycle)

1. Growth: Allow polymers to replicate in chemostat with fresh monomers and energy (ATP). Dilution rate = 0.5 h⁻¹ (half of population replaced each hour).
2. Mutation: The error‑prone polymerase introduces random substitutions (1% per genome). Also occasional insertions/deletions (0.1%).
3. Selection: Use optical sorting (FACS) to retain the top 10% of individuals based on fitness. The rest are washed out.
4. Replenish: Add fresh monomers and polymerase. Go to step 1.

3.4 Environmental variation (optional)

To encourage open‑ended evolution, change the fitness function periodically (e.g., every 100 generations). For example: first toxin A, then toxin B, then a different computational task. This drives the evolution of meta‑learning (ability to adapt quickly).

🔬 4. Monitoring & Analysis

· Sequencing: Periodically extract polymer DNA (or directly sequence the HDP using nanopore) to track evolutionary lineages.
· Fitness tracking: Record the average fitness over time. Expect a logarithmic increase (steady improvement, no plateau).
· Phenotype analysis: Use AFM and FRET to characterize folding states of evolved polymers.
· Emergent behaviors: Look for speciation (clusters of similar sequences), altruism (cooperation), multicellularity (aggregation), or chemical language.
